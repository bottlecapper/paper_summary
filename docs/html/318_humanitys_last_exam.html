Humanity’s Last Exam

Center for AI Safety and Scale AI · Oct 2026 · ~4-minute overview · Paper

Summary

  • Purpose: HLE measures advanced academic knowledge and reasoning through closed-ended questions, extending measurement beyond saturated benchmarks.

  • Scope: 2,500 public questions span over a hundred subjects. Around 14% require images; 24% are multiple-choice. A private set checks overfitting; its size is not reported.

  • Task Design: Experts write original questions or non-trivial syntheses with known answers. Candidate questions must defeat frontier models, then pass iterative expert review.

  • Time and Cost: The prize pool is $500,000 USD. Estimated question-review effort is 3-5 minutes; human solving time and highest-scoring-model wall-clock runtime are not reported.

  • Evaluation Method: An o3-mini judge compares final answers with reference answers, accepting equivalent forms. Scoring is binary, without partial credit; report accuracy and root-mean-square (RMS) error between stated confidence and empirical accuracy.

Dataset Creation

  • Expert Sourcing: Nearly 1000 experts from over 500 institutions across 50 countries contribute; most are professors, researchers, or graduate degree holders.

    Source Excerpts
    • HLE is a global collaborative effort, with questions from nearly 1000 subject expert contributors affiliated with over 500 institutions across 50 countries – comprised mostly of professors, researchers, and graduate degree holders.

  • Incentives: Accepted questions earn co-authorship; prizes encourage expert participation.

    Source Excerpts
    • The $500,000 USD pool awards $5,000 USD for each of the top 50 questions and $500 USD for each of the next 500 questions, as determined by organizers.

  • Task Design:

    • Coverage: Questions cover mathematics, sciences, humanities, and specialized fields.

      Source Excerpts
      • HUMANITY’S LAST EXAM (HLE) consists of 2,500 challenging questions across over a hundred subjects.
      • The abstract describes 2,500 questions across dozens of subjects, including mathematics, humanities, and the natural sciences. Section B.4 introduces its list as the top fifty most popular subjects in HLE: Economies, Ecology, Artificial Intelligence, Musicology, Philosophy, Neuroscience, Law, Art History, Biochemistry, Astronomy, Classics, Chess, Chemical Engineering, Microbiology, Classical Ballet, Materials Science, Poetry, Quantum Mechanics, Aerospace Engineering, Civil Engineering, Mechanical Engineering, Geography, Robotics, Data Science, Molecular Biology, Statistics, Immunology, Education, Logic, Computational Biology, Psychology, English Literature, Machine Learning, Puzzle, Cultural Studies, Marine Biology, Archaeology, and Biophysics.

    • Answer Formats: Use short exact-match answers or at least five multiple-choice options; questions usually require graduate-level expertise.

      Source Excerpts
      • HLE is multi-modal, featuring questions that are either text-only or accompanied by an image reference, and includes both multiple-choice and exact-match questions for automated answer verification.
      • Answers are kept short and easily verifiable for exact-match questions to support automatic grading. Both formats are suitable for automated grading.

    • Submission Contents: Submit the question, answer specification, detailed solution, subject, and contributor identity.

      Source Excerpts
      • Each question submission includes several required components: the question text itself, answer specifications (either an an exact-match answer, or multiple-choice options with the correct answer marked), detailed rationale explaining the solution, academic subject, and contributor name and institutional affiliation to maintain accountability and accuracy.

  • Example Tasks:

    Source Excerpts

    • Example Task: Figure 2 – Ecology (hummingbird sesamoid).

      The task instruction is: “Hummingbirds within Apodiformes uniquely have a bilaterally paired oval bone, a sesamoid embedded in the caudolateral portion of the expanded, cruciate aponeurosis of insertion of m. depressor caudae. How many paired tendons are supported by this sesamoid bone? Answer with a number.”

Quality Control

Human Review

  • Review Approach:

    • First-round refinement: Graduate-qualified experts use standardized rubrics, provide feedback, and iteratively revise questions; each receives 1-3 reviews. Scores range from 0 to 5, with an unsure option.
      Source Excerpts
      • Reviewers possess a graduate degree (eg. Master’s, PhD, JD, etc.) in their fields. They select submissions in their domain, grading them against standardized rubrics and offering feedback when applicable.
      • The first round focuses on iteratively refining submissions, with each question receiving between 1-3 reviews. Experts score questions, provide feedback, and help authors improve them, similar to academic peer review. Round-one scores run from 0 (discard) to 5 (top-notch), with an unsure option.
    • Second-round approval: Organizers and trained reviewers consider questions and prior feedback, then approve the best submissions. Scores range from 0 to 6.
      Source Excerpts
      • In the second round, good and outstanding questions from the first round are identified and approved by organizers and reviewers to be included in the final HLE dataset. Organizers train reviewers selected for thorough first-round feedback; reviewers are asked to grade both the question and look at feedback from round 1 reviewers. Round-two scores run from 0 to 6.
      • Figure 4 summarizes model-failure filtering, iterative expert refinement, and individual manual approval.

    • Community feedback: A public review and bug bounty identify label and major question-statement errors; organizers verify reports with author input.

      Source Excerpts
      • Following release, public review solicits community corrections. Initial reviewers were not expected to verify the full accuracy of each provided solution rationale if it would take more than five minutes, instead focusing on whether the question aligns with guidelines.

    • Sample-solving audits: Students solve questions fully; organizers, authors, and auditors resolve flagged errors by consensus.

      Source Excerpts
      • Students from top US universities fully solve a sample. Flagged errors are routed among organizers, original question authors, and auditors until consensus. These audits inform dataset refinement. Search-flagged questions are manually checked and removed if easily found online.
    • Expert disagreement: Two samples of 200 questions each, with author rebuttals, support revisions. Estimated public-set disagreement is 15.4%; a targeted biology/chemistry/health review finds approximately 18%.
      Source Excerpts
      • The audits involve expert reviewers from leading research universities in the United States, with a rebuttal phase from the original question authors for any disagreements. The first audit identifies patterns of imprecision; similar questions are revised or removed before a new sample is audited.
      • The highlighted The Need for Multiple Experts: explains why multiple reviewers and author rebuttals matter: specialized evidence, such as an old paper, can resolve a dissent. On the health-focused subset, flagging any single dissent raises disagreement from 18% to 25%. Disagreement is not a confirmed error rate.

  • Quality Dimensions:

    • Validity and Clarity: Questions should be precise, unambiguous, solvable, and non-searchable, ensuring models cannot rely on memorization or simple retrieval methods.

      Source Excerpts
      • For psychology/philosophy, reviewers check whether the rationale contains some reference to a book, paper or standard theories. Make sure questions about law are time-bounded. An example is “as of 2024”. If there is some non-standard jargon for the topic/field, it needs to be explained.
      • Open-ended prompts such as “Give a proof of. . . ”; “Explain why. . . ”; “Provide a theory that explains. . . ” are generally unsuitable. Subjective interpretation and morality/ethics questions are excluded.
      • Question with numerical answers should have results approximated to max 2-3 decimals. Questions that can be converted to text should be converted; clear LaTeX formatting can reveal that a model actually knows the answer. Explanations should include at least some details or reference. If the rationale is unclear or not detailed, ask in the review to expand a bit.

    • Multiple-Choice Interpretation: Judge the most plausible provided option, rather than demand an open-ended perfect solution.

      Source Excerpts
      • Researchers leverage the multiple-choice format with the objective of identifying the most plausible answer among the provided options. Reviewers compare relative merits rather than treating the task as an open-ended search for a perfect solution.

    • Genuine Difficulty: Reject easy questions missed by models and artificial difficulty caused by unavailable calculator/code tools.

      Source Excerpts
      • The second rubric labels a category Easy questions models got wrong. It includes basic/searchable questions and questions that are artificially difficult because of large calculator-dependent computations or running/rendering code. Evaluated models lack those tools, creating an artificial difficulty bar. Research papers, journals, and books are acceptable sources; simple online lookup is the exclusion criterion.

Auto Judge

  • Difficulty Screening: Exact-match candidates must stump all screening models; multiple-choice candidates may be solved by at most one. GPT-4o judges screening answers; search-assisted successes trigger manual searchability checks.
    Source Excerpts
    • Section B.1 specifies that exact-match questions must stump all models, while multiple-choice questions must stump all but one model to account for potential lucky guesses. Section 3.2 describes the multiple-choice goal as models averaging below random guessing.
    • Screening uses “Reasoning” and “Final Answer” response formatting and an automated GPT-4O judge against submitted answers. More than 70,000 attempts yield approximately 13,000 questions for human review.
    • A question is potentially searchable if a model with search tools answered correctly, but answered incorrectly without search. GPT-4o mini/GPT-4o search and Perplexity Sonar supply this signal; human auditors make removal decisions.

Model Evaluation

Human Review

  • Role: Humans review reference questions and answers; no separate human scoring of model responses or judge–human agreement study is reported.

Auto Judge

  • Answer Judge: o3-mini checks answer equivalence against the supplied reference.

    Source Excerpts
    • The evaluation prompt requests explanation, final answer, and confidence. Models without system-prompt support receive it as a separate user prompt. The judge is o3-mini-2025-01-31 with structured decoding; it extracts extracted_final_answer, reasoning, correctness, and confidence.
    • The judge accounts for equivalent formats (e.g., decimals vs. fractions or estimations). It compares answers rather than independently re-solving the problem. Correctness is yes/no, allowing small numerical error; no final answer extracts as None, and missing confidence defaults to 100.
    • The structured-response example in Section C.1.1 uses a half-angle identity to establish equivalence between two trigonometric forms and returns yes.

  • Headline Results: In the paper’s early-2025 text-only evaluation, o3-mini (high) scores 13.4% accuracy, DeepSeek-R1 8.5%, o1 7.8%, and GPT-4o 2.3%. Current leaderboard.

    Source Excerpts
    • Table 1 accuracy / RMS calibration error (%): GPT-4o 2.7 / 89; Grok 2 3.0 / 87; Claude 3.5 Sonnet 4.1 / 84; Gemini 1.5 Pro 4.6 / 88; Gemini 2.0 Flash Thinking 6.6 / 82; o1 8.0 / 83. Text-only DeepSeek-R1 scores 8.5 / 73; text-only o3-mini (high) scores 13.4 / 80. The text-only rows use a different population from full-dataset rows.
    • Table 2 gives the shared text-only population used in the visible comparison; its remaining baselines are Grok 2 3.2%, Claude 3.5 Sonnet 4.3%, Gemini 1.5 Pro 4.6%, and Gemini 2.0 Flash Thinking 6.6%.

  • Confidence Calibration: Models frequently provide incorrect answers with high confidence on HLE, failing to recognize when questions exceed their capabilities.

    Source Excerpts
    • Models give confidence from 0% to 100%. A well-calibrated model’s stated confidence should match its actual accuracy – for example, achieving 50% accuracy on questions where it claims 50% confidence.

Key Findings

  • Guessing and Variability: Small score changes near zero need caution.

    Source Excerpts
    • models can inconsistently guess the right answer or guess worse than random chance for multiple choice questions. Such questions remain rather than being repeatedly filtered; low non-zero accuracy partly reflects inference randomness and the multiple-choice floor.

  • Evaluation Landscape: HLE combines broad coverage with expert authorship and difficult questions.

    Source Excerpts
    • Related work spans scientific and mathematical reasoning, code generation, and general-purpose human assistance. HLE complements open-ended evaluations. Reasoning models improve accuracy using substantially more completion tokens; token counts measure compute, not wall-clock time.

Special Design

  • Closed-Ended Frontier: Known, verifiable answers permit automated grading at unusually advanced knowledge levels.

    Source Excerpts
    • HLE is designed as a multi-modal benchmark at the frontier of human knowledge, designed to be the final closed-ended academic benchmark of its kind with broad subject coverage. Each question has a known solution that is unambiguous and easily verifiable, but cannot be quickly answered via internet retrieval.

  • Research Experience: Contributor experiments supply knowledge beyond easily indexed sources.

    Source Excerpts
    • HLE is intentionally designed to include questions based on insights from the direct, hands-on experiments of its contributors. This design captures knowledge gained from direct research experiences, which is often difficult to verify through standard literature searches or by external reviewers.

  • Discouraging Lucky Answers: Adjust question parameters to reduce correct guesses with faulty reasoning.

    Source Excerpts
    • When LLMs provide correct answers with faulty reasoning, authors are encouraged to modify question parameters, such as the number of answer choices, to discourage false positives.

Impact

  • Implications: A shared academic benchmark can support capability tracking and research/policy discussion. Iterative expert review and held-out questions help maintain its measurement value.

  • Limitations: HLE tests structured academic problems rather than open-ended research or creative problem-solving abilities, making it a focused measure of technical knowledge and reasoning.

    Source Excerpts
    • High performance alone does not establish autonomous research or artificial general intelligence. The initial review’s limited solution verification and substantial expert disagreement also constrain interpretation.

  • Continued Refinement: The paper proposes HLE-ROLLING as a dynamic fork.

    Source Excerpts
    • This version will be regularly updated to address community feedback and integrate new questions.
